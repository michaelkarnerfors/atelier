# When your writing tool starts fighting back

*There is a better way to work on long manuscripts.*
*And it does not have to cost you anything but some time.*

---

You know the moment.

You are deep in a scene. The dialogue is finally clicking, the pacing is right,
the page has _movement_. And then your word processor decides to have "fun"
with the layout — a style resets, a paragraph changes font, a heading
decides it belongs to a different chapter — and you spend the next twenty
minutes in formatting hell instead of adding to your story.

What about that document you have been working at for two years — it is 80,000
words by now. It opens slow, and saves with that ominous little pause that makes
you swallow hard. You back it up by emailing it to yourself, which you know is
not really a backup, but it is the best — the only — system you have. You try
not to think about what happens if the file breaks and cannot be opened, or when
the next version of the word processor comes out and starts having opinions
about your old files.

Or when your editor sends back a copy of chapter twelve with
tracked changes, but you have a different version open on your laptop, and you
cannot quite remember which one has the ending you actually wanted.

Or when your computer says "Disk Error", and makes a horrible noise.

Or when...

These are not failures of discipline or organisation. It is what happens
when you try to write something long and complicated in a tool that was
designed for short office documents. The word processor is not your enemy — it
is just not built for long-haul work.

---

## Separating the writing from the formatting

There is a different approach, and it has been used by publishing houses,
academic journals, and newspapers for decades. The idea is called
"Content First" and is quite simple: keep your writing separate from your
formatting.

How does it work?

You write in plain-text, just words. No font menus, no style panels, no
formatting toolbar competing for your attention. Each chapter lives in its own
file, the way each page of a handwritten manuscript occupies its own sheet of
paper. When you are ready to see the finished result, you run a single command,
and everything is assembled into the output, such as a PDF, an EPUB, and a
Word document.

A typesetting engine is doing the work under the hood for you. These engines
have been around for decades, since the 1980s, used in particular by academic
publishers and university presses. They are mature, and good at their
job. And because the formatting is defined once in a template and applied
consistently, it never surprises you.

---

## Your manuscript, thirty years from now

Another benefit of the "Content First" approach is that plain-text is the most
durable format in computing. Your chapters will open on any computer, in any
editor, on any operating system — today, next week, three decades from now.
They are not tied to a subscription, a company, or a file format that might one
day go unsupported.

There is something else — because your manuscript is plain-text files, you can
use the same version control system that software developers use to track every
change they make to their code. Every draft; every deleted scene; every
revision, all of them retrievable. You can see exactly what your manuscript
looked like six months ago, compare any two versions, and roll back a change
that turned out to be a mistake. If you work with collaborators, you can see who
changed what and when.

"Content First" thus allows you to have a reliable time machine for your
manuscript — one that also keeps an off-site backup of everything you have ever
written, on a free private repository, safe from hardware failures and theft.

## How can you write this way?

Let us introduce you to **Atelier** — a free, open-source writing studio
built around the notion that...

- You should be _writing_, not fighting the text to make it behave
- Content and presentation should be kept separate
- Your creation should be yours, and not leashed to a pricey tool

Atelier gathers several free professional tools and lets you
use them, without restrictions.

- XeLaTeX/MiKTeX for professional typesetting
- Vale for advanced prose-linting
- Git for revision control and backup

...all available with a single command.

---

## It is not for everyone

This approach has a cost, and it is worth saying plainly: setting it up takes
time — likely an afternoon, possibly longer. You will need to get comfortable
with a terminal. The first build might feel slow. If there is a snag, the error
comes from the typesetting engine, not a friendly dialogue box.

So if your current setup is working and the word processor is not getting in
your way, this is probably not the right tool for you. There is no need to fix
what is not broken.

But if you write long-form work and live with the quiet anxiety of that single
massive file, or if you want professionally typeset output without paying for
expensive layout software, or if you want a setup that is completely under your
own control and costs nothing to use — Atelier might be worth your afternoon.
