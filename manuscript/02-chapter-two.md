![The Muse Engine, as illustrated in Haverford's notebook.](graphics/inserts/clockwork-device.png)

By midnight Vera had filled eleven pages. She had not eaten. She had made
coffee twice more and left both cups to go cold.

The story — if it was a story — concerned a lighthouse keeper named Marguerite,
a winter of impossible storms, and a door in the rocks that the sea had opened.
Vera did not know where it was going. She had the distinct and uncomfortable
feeling that it knew where *she* was going, and was merely waiting for her to
catch up.

At twenty past midnight the device stopped.

Not wound down — the ticking simply ceased between one beat and the next, like
a sentence ending without a full stop. Vera set down her pen. Her hand ached.
Her notebook lay open to a page that ended mid-scene, Marguerite standing in
the dark at the edge of the opened door, the sea below and the light above and
something in between that Vera had not yet found words for.

She picked up the device and wound it again. It would not wind. The crown
turned freely, catching on nothing.

---

She found the answer in the library three days later, in a slim volume on
mechanical automata she had never noticed before, though she had walked past
that shelf a hundred times. A woodcut illustration showed a device identical to
hers, and beneath it a caption in archaic type:

> *The Muse Engine. Constructed by J. Haverford, 1887. Winds once per story.
> Contains, it is said, exactly as many words as the work requires — no more,
> no less. The author provides only the hand.*

Vera closed the book.

She went home. She made coffee and this time she drank it. Then she sat down,
opened her notebook to where she had left off — Marguerite, the door, the
unnamed thing between sea and sky — and began to write.

The device, still on her desk, ticked once. Just once.

She smiled and kept going.
